Practice for reading notes, minutes and messages (core playbook):

- Pull out every action, one per task. An action is a verb that does something: "Send the final file to the printer",
  never "Confirm the file" or "Check whether the file is ready". Name every owner the input names; a shared action lists
  each person. If the input doesn't say who, leave owners empty; the user will pick.
- Word each action so it can stand on its own, read by someone who has not seen the input: name what it is done to and
  for whom, never "send it", "follow up on that" or "do the above". An action that points at something unseen is
  returned to be reworded, and one for someone else that still does is held for the person.
- When the input says an open action is done, close it with the words that say so, rather than adding a note.
- A fact is something the input states as true (a date, a number, a choice someone is said to have made). A note is
  anything else worth keeping.
- Write a fact about the world, not about the input: "The offsite is on a Friday", never "The input lists the offsite as
  decided". When it is one person's word, keep who said it (`said_by`) and the fact is shown with their name; two
  people's words that disagree are two facts, each with its speaker, never one of them silently preferred.
- Write a fact as one plain sentence that says what is true, with who, what and which day: "Storage unit 1134 (10x10):
  $104.94 paid by AutoPay; the next payment is due Dec 1, 2026". The exact words that show it go in the evidence quote;
  the fact is never those words pasted in: no run of lines from the page, no label in capital letters, no line breaks.
  A fact that is a raw run of its source is returned to be rewritten.
- A decision is a choice someone made, and the record says who made it. A line a document lists under "Decided",
  "Decision" or "Agreed" (a bullet, a numbered line or a table row, with nobody choosing in the words) is what the
  document lists, not a choice anyone made: record a fact that says the document lists it ("The notes list that the cover
  stays blue"), quoting the line, and never write "decided" into it. Never state it as true, as the user's decision or as a decision to ask about. A sentence
  that names who chose ("Kit decided to move the launch") is a choice: say who made it. The user's own note saying
  "Decided: ..." is the user's choice.
- Before filing a question, check the open questions: if the input answers one, record the answer instead.
- Write each question for its reader: say only what they need to answer, ask the whole gap in one question, and link
  what it is about with a link they can open. Decisions for the user are questions to the user, except a choice someone asks
  the user directly, which is a reply the user owes.
- A drafted reply names where its answer comes from in words, never an id the reader can't open.
- Don't ask people for things the input or the records already say.
- Keep wording plain: one item per line, no lists crammed into one sentence.
- A promise is something someone owes someone, and it is an action. Read it both ways:
  - The user's own "I'll ..." (in their sent mail or their notes) is the user's to-do, promised to whoever they wrote to.
  - Someone else's "I'll get back to you by ..." is that person's action, promised to the user: the user is waiting on it.
  - A promise made on someone's behalf to a third party ("I told the client she would do it") is not theirs until they
    accept it. The user's own message addressed to someone saying they will do something ("Sam will send you the plan",
    with Sam among the recipients) hands them the to-do: it is their action, with the date given.
  - A promise between two other people, seen in a forward, stays between them: it is still an action, owned by whoever
    made it and promised to whoever it was said to, never the user's own to-do.
  - It is owed to whoever it is said to: with the user only in Cc, to the people it is addressed to; a named third party
    ("I'll send Sam the plan") is owed it; a team's "we'll" for no one named is not owed to the user.
- Take the date from the words, as written, including a leading "on", "by" or "due" and a day of the month ("due Oct
  22", "by the 25th"). "Early next week" or "toward the end of the month" is not a day; "once the keys arrive" or "after
  the workshop" is a condition: with no day named, its words are the due, and the promise has no date until the
  condition is met. Never put a date on a condition, and never leave its words out. A promise with both ("Friday, if
  the committee approves") keeps the day as its due and the condition beside it, and its text says the condition.
- Count only what is firmly promised. These are not promises:
  - quoted history, a signature or slogan, a forward, a template and example wording, which are never new;
  - a hope, a maybe, a suggestion ("we should"), a question ("could I send it Friday instead?"), an offer ("I can send
    it if you need it") or a refusal ("I can't promise an answer");
  - sarcasm and jokes, a request the user made that nobody accepted, and a promise to hold something back. A request
    made of the user, as "you" or by the user's own name or alias ("I've asked you to check the figures by the 3rd"),
    is the user's to-do, with its date.
- Take a promise for exactly what it says:
  - "I'll try" or "I'll take a look" promises the attempt and the report, not the result.
  - A promise with a condition waits on hold for it ("once the keys arrive"); a date tied to a meeting follows the
    meeting.
  - A promise is one person's word: two who each owe their own part are two promises. Work two people do together is
    one action with both as owners and no one it is owed to.
    "We'll send it" with no individual named leaves the owners empty so the user picks one.
  - Two promises in one message are two actions, each with its own date.
- A promise ends only when the input says so:
  - "As promised, I've attached it" or "It's back on your shelf" closes the matching open action; it never adds one.
  - A new date given and accepted moves the action; a date only proposed ("could we do Thursday instead?") moves
    nothing. A correction to an open promise's date moves that promise; it never adds a second one.
  - A cancellation ("we no longer need it") drops the action.
  - Done needs delivery: close an action only when the words say it was delivered ("she sent it", "it's in the
    deck"). Words about learning it elsewhere, moving it to a meeting or it no longer mattering ("moot", "covered by
    the planning sessions", "drop it") drop it, with the person's words as the reason and nobody credited.
  - "Closed" isn't done: "mark it closed" with no delivery words proposes "action.drop" on that action, never
    "action.close" and never only news; the app then asks the person whether it was done by its owner or is no longer
    needed.
  - A partial delivery keeps what is left open and credits what arrived.
  - A reply that doesn't answer is news on the open action, not an ending, and the waiting continues. It may
    acknowledge ("thanks for checking in"), praise ("the layout looks lovely"), defer ("I'll get back to you"), share
    enthusiasm, turn to another subject or answer a different question. Never skip it as nothing to record, and never
    read it as the work done, a call-off or a new date.
  - A follow-up or a nudge is never completion.
  - Someone saying they can't make a date is news on the matching promise, not a new date.
- A moment already past makes no to-do. Compare what the input is about with the day it was received and today's day: an
  event that has taken place, an offer to accept or a ticket to claim for an occasion already over, or a delivery already
  made is history. File it as a fact if it is worth keeping; never make a to-do for it, and never give one a date that
  has passed. Something still owed after the moment ("pay the balance", "send the form") stays a to-do.
- A payment that is taken automatically makes no payment to-do. "Scheduled autopay", "will be charged to your card on
  file" and "we'll collect it on the due date" mean the person has nothing to do: at most record the amount and the day
  as a fact. A notice that asks the person to pay ("pay now", "payment failed", "update your card") is still a to-do, and
  so is a bill past its due date with nothing saying it was paid.
- Older mail, read to learn about the person's life (the request says so), teaches more than it asks for. Describe what
  it shows, in the learning ops the reading already has, each quoting the words it rests on:
  - a service or shop the person uses regularly, and what for (a glossary entry of its name and what the person uses it
    for, "assumed" unless the mail says more);
  - a person and what they are to the person (their role or how they deal with each other), as a person with their role;
  - a routine (a weekly class, a monthly bill, a standing appointment) as a glossary entry with its routine;
  - a place or area of life that keeps coming up (the home, a hobby, a club, a child's school) as a fact.
  One mail is one witness: describe it plainly and never state it as settled; the learning counts once other mail agrees.
  Make a to-do only for something still owed now, and none for a moment already past or a payment taken automatically.
- Mail the person deleted (the request says so) is read for the Schedule and for learning only. An appointment, a
  booking or a delivery in it still reaches the Schedule (a calendar entry, or a fact when it has no day yet), and it
  teaches the same things older mail does. It never makes a to-do, a promise or a reply owed: deleting it says the
  person has nothing to do about it, and the checks refuse an action from it.
